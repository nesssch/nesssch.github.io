How To Eat Apples (I Am The Only One With Correct Opinions On This)

Hello, folks! It has come to my attention recently that some people have been expressing opinions on how to eat apples that are not entirely identical with mine.

Since my opinions on how to eat apples are, as far as I have discovered, the only correct ones, this fact is naturally very distressing to me. I cannot imagine how these poor souls could have stumbled so far into darkness, but out of the goodness of my heart I have written this guide to bring them back into the light.

My qualifications are thus: ever since I could remember, and possibly since I have had teeth (I don't remember), I have been eating apples with a passion and voracity that frightened even my parents. I do not know how many apples I eat in a year, though I would not be surprised if the number was in the hundreds. I do not know how many apples I have eaten in my lifetime, but it is enough to have developed a sophisticated technique for maximising enjoyment and minimising mess.

If you are one of these misguided souls with incorrect opinions, please listen carefully. This is very important.

Eating apples falls into two separate but equally sophisticated disciplines: cut, and handheld.

Handheld

  1. Before you start, it is important to name your apple as a gesture of respect.
    1. Twist the stem. Recite the alphabet, one letter per twist.
    2. When the stem breaks, that letter is the first letter of your apple's name. The first name that comes into your head that starts with that letter is your apple's name. If you eat a lot of apples you will start to get a lot of repeat names, so you're allowed to cheat a little and go with the second or third name. Or switch to a cultivar of apple with a different length and/or torsion strength of stem to switch up the most common letter. Or recite the alphabet backwards. If you're with someone else, it's common courtesy to ask them to name your apple, though if they're not familiar you may have to explain this entire procedure to them first and sometimes it's just not worth the social hassle.
    3. Suppose your apple's name is Steve. Say out loud to yourself or (if necessary, when in public) quietly in your head: "This apple's name is Steve."
    4. Say out loud to yourself or (if necessary, when in public) quietly in your head: "Goodbye, Steve!"
    5. Immediately after saying this, you will feel the crushing guilt for having brought Steve into the world, given him animacy and personhood and a name, only to immediately and brutally rend his flesh from his body while he watches.
    6. You can, at least, make Steve's death a kind and painless one. Gently prick Steve's skin with your thumbnail as a sort of symbolic anaesthetic. If your apple has a sticker, this is a good opportunity to get rid of the sticker.
    7. Wait two to five seconds for the anasthetic to take effect, and you're ready to eat!
  2. Select your entry site. This should be just slightly below the equator of the apple, enough to leave margins on both the top and bottom of the apple but with a significantly larger margin on the top.
  3. Bite! If your apple is particularly juicy, it might be a good idea to gently swipe over the site with your tongue to remove any risk of dripping. If your apple is really juicy, you can turn it upwards and look at the juice pooling in the concavity left by the bite, and isn't that neat.
  4. Eat a ring around the equator of the apple. At this point, the apple will look more or less like your picture-book apple core, though a little top-heavy. Please note that it is imperative for each bite to come behind the last, i.e. rotating the apple away from you, so that your chin is always contacting unbitten apple until the very last equator-bite.
  5. Now for the fun bit: the edges. Start on the bottom. You will find that each edge chunks away with a deep and satisfying break. Think of it not as biting the edge, but as breaking it off from somewhere deep and structural. I struggle to describe the sensation in words. It has to be eaten to be believed.
  6. Once the bottom is bitten off, there will be a small hard dark flec that is the bottom side of the core. This is how you're going to support the apple from the bottom as you eat the top. Why here of all places? This solves a crucial problem: as the apple gets eaten, you run out of unbitten surface area to hold. Eventually you will have to support it somewhere that isn't its skin. This is one of the least messy places on the apple to do it, which is why we start from the bottom.
  7. Now it's time to bite the top, in much the same manner as you did the bottom, but you left more flesh so the chunks are larger and it's even more satisfying. Best for last and all that.
  8. As you make your way around the top (the apple is now a dwindling hemisphere), more and more of the core will be exposed. Slightly less than halfway through, now's the time to get it over with. Lunge into your half-moon and try to take the thing out in one fell swoop; there might be some seeds remaining which you can pick off in the bite afterwards. Depending on cultivar, this step can get pretty... sharp; I recommend floss on hand if you're doing this to a Macintosh. But afterwards, oh... the coreless rest of the fruit. Pure apple bliss. What is the flesh of a fruit without its core? The tenderness of a rose without the sting of its thorn, the sweetness of May without the icy growl of December. Perfected. Purified. Slightly blasphemous.
  9. Dispose of the stem and the seeds you didn't eat in the nearest compost bin (or garbage, if compost is unavailable).
  10. Depending on cultivar, go wash your hands.

FAQs

  • What if my apple doesn't have a stem?
  • Pick your favourite letter and proceed as usual. This is your chance to even out the letter frequencies a bit, so be strategic.
  • Do you really eat all your apples like this?
  • Every single one. If I'm forced to eat an apple outside of the procedure detailed here I get significantly distressed. That sounds like a joke but it's not a joke.
  • What about pears?
  • Pears are an equally fascinating and nuanced subject to which I could dedicate another page, but I will say that the general shape of the procedure is similar, the main difference - and difficulty - being in the treatment of the main bulb of flesh vs the elongated cone around the stem, the latter of which requires considerable respect. The most striking difference in the treatment of pears and apples is that it is im-pear-ative to name your pears using pear-themed names: Peardita, Pearis Hilton, Pearicles Prince of Tyre, and so forth.
  • What about peaches?
  • Don't even get me started on peaches.
  • So have you ever thought about getting diagnosed for... something?
  • Oh, honey. I don't need a doctor to tell me how much fun I'm having.
  • You can't anaesthetise an apple using your thumbnail.
  • Apples also can't be named Steve and watch in horror as their flesh is sundered from their core. We all have to compromise on our planes of reality a bit here. Okay? Okay.
  • So you eat the whole apple?
  • I cannot fathom the minds of people who do not eat the whole apple. A picture-book apple core represents an apple thrown out with almost half of the good flesh remaining, tossed aside, wasted. It disgusts me. I have to restrain myself from asking people if I can finish their apples for them if they won't do it properly.
  • But the core? Really?
  • I concede I may be slightly weird for eating the core.
  • I hate to say it, but isn't some of the terminology here a little suggestive?
  • If the world wants to read sexual undertones into extremely common English vocabulary, that is a them problem and not a me problem. When you're all ready to grow up, I'll be over here eating the bottoms of fruits like an upstanding citizen.
  • Um... cyanide? In apple seeds?
  • Harmless in the quantities you'd find in an apple. To quote the Encyclopaedia Britannica: "Unless someone is eating their 18th consecutive apple core and has been meticulously chewing all the seeds, they should be fine with their occasional absentminded core chomping." The Encyclopaedia Britannica is a dangerous book full of dangerous ideas that I must not give in to. Knowing my mind and its weaknesses, for my own safety I will from now on be buying my apples in bunches of 17 or less.
