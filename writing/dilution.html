People generally think of two things when they hear the word "model": statistical models, or mechanistic models.

Let's say you're trying to predict the population of deer over time.

One approach you could take would be: collect a lot of data, and do a regression. For example, maybe you've determined that your deer population fits an exponential distribution over time with base abc and exponent xyz. You can then use this to make numerical predictions about the population size over time. This is a "statistical model."

The other approach is to identify the underlying mechanism that is driving the population size, and translate it into math. For example, it might be a sensible assumption that each female deer has a constant, average number of female offspring which survive to reproduction. If you're making no other assumptions about predation or carrying capacity or whatever, then mathematically your assumption translates to: your deer population follows an exponential distribution. This is a "mechanistic model".

What is "biodiversity", even? There are a number of different definitions.

Species richness: the number of distinct species in an ecosystem. For example, an ecosystem with only flamingos and kakapos has less species richness than a system with flamingos, kakapos, quetzals, and roseate spoonbills.

Species evenness: how evenly-distributed those species are. For example, a system where quetzals make up 90% of the bird population has less species richness than a system with 30% flamingos, 10% kakapos, 40% quetzals and 20% roseate spoonbills.

The Shannon-Weiner index: a method of combining richness and evenness. tk: this has some neat connections to information theory.
