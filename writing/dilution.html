People generally think of two things when they hear the word "model": statistical models, or mechanistic models.

Let's say you're trying to predict the population of deer over time.

One approach you could take would be: collect a lot of data, and do a regression. For example, maybe you've determined that your deer population fits an exponential distribution over time with base abc and exponent xyz. You can then use this to make numerical predictions about the population size over time. This is a "statistical model."

The other approach is to identify the underlying mechanism that is driving the population dynamics, and translate it into math. For example, it might be a sensible assumption that each female deer has a constant, average number of female offspring which survive to reproduction. If you're making no other assumptions about predation or carrying capacity or whatever, then mathematically your assumption translates to: your deer population follows an exponential distribution. This is a "mechanistic model".

controversial hypothesis

The phrase "dilution effect", in the context of disease ecology, was first used - as far as I can tell - in 2001. (tk. track down the Ostfeld citation). Since then, we haven't stopped arguing about it. The drama peaked in 2013 or so and has slowly simmered down since. I won't share my more gleeful, schadenfreud'isch recountings here, but the immediate policy implications of the dilution effect gave the debate a faintly political cast, with implicit questions about science and policy and publishing bias.

It didn't help that, because biodiversity and disease are both concepts that can look so many different ways at so many different scales, the dilution effect is notoriously slippery to pin down. Empirical and modelling papers alike need to start with a very careful description of what their definitions and assumptions are.

Nowadays, the focus has shifted from "does this effect exist in general" to "how do we predict whether this effect would materialise in a given ecosystem" to "how do community dynamics affect disease dynamics". IMO, this is a much more productive question. I don't think we'd ever be able to answer whether something as many-faceted and malleable as the dilution effect "exists in general".

What is "biodiversity", even? There are a number of different definitions.

Species richness: the number of distinct species in an ecosystem. For example, an ecosystem with only flamingos and kakapos has less species richness than a system with flamingos, kakapos, quetzals, and roseate spoonbills.

Species evenness: how evenly-distributed those species are. For example, a system where quetzals make up 90% of the bird population has less species evenness than a system with 30% flamingos, 10% kakapos, 40% quetzals and 20% roseate spoonbills.

The Shannon-Weiner index: a method of combining richness and evenness. tk: this has some neat connections to information theory.

...and many more!

My research focuses on species richness: specifically, what happens when you add one new species to an ecosystem.

The many ways of defining biodiversity are one of the reasons why the dilution effect is so difficult to precisely pin down.

What is disease, even? There are a number of different ways to quantify it.

Disease incidence: how many individuals have the disease.

Disease prevalence: the proportion of individuals which have the disease. This is equal to disease incidence divided by your population size.

R0 (pronounced "arr nought"): how many new infections a newly infectious individual will cause, on average, in a disease-free population. (tk: there are other definitions of R0!)

Different disease metrics will give you different results, and may or may not be relevant to the system you're studying. (tk)
